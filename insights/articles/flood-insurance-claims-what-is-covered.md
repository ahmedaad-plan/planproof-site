One thing to say first. This article describes what was reported and decided after the UAE floods of 16 April 2024, from public sources listed at the end. It is not legal advice and it is not financial advice. Whether a particular policy covers a particular loss is a question for the policy wording and for a licensed broker. What we can do is set out the pattern, because the pattern is what a continuity plan has to be ready for.

## The short history

The storm of 16 April 2024 dropped roughly a year's rain in a day. Within six days the Central Bank of the UAE, which also supervises insurers, issued a notice to banks and insurers. Banks were asked to let affected customers defer personal-loan and car-loan instalments for up to six months with no fees or added principal. The same notice said storm damage to homes and vehicles is covered where the owner holds a loss-and-damage (comprehensive) policy, that insurers are responsible for settling such losses, and that anyone with a dispute should take it to Sanadak, the central bank's ombudsman unit, which had opened only six weeks before the storm.

Then came the volume. Brokers described roughly twelve years' worth of motor claims arriving within a few days. An aggregator said insurers that usually saw one or two hundred motor claims a month received 1,500 in a single month. Industry estimates of damaged vehicles ran from 30,000–50,000 to more than 100,000, and the regulator published no count. Towing capacity ran out; workshops were still clearing the backlog a year later, and mechanics said more cars were written off than repaired. By early 2025 most flood claims had been assessed and paid, typically within about three months of the event.

The money, when it was finally counted, was mostly in property, not cars. Estimates of the insured loss grew from about a billion dollars in the first weeks to roughly three billion by September 2024, with motor only about a tenth of it; a rating agency later put it at up to two and a half billion, most of it passed to international reinsurers.

## What was covered: motor

Motor insurance in the UAE runs on a unified policy. Two things about it decided most of the April 2024 outcomes.

The first is the difference between third-party and comprehensive. Third-party cover pays for damage you do to other people and their property. It never pays for your own vehicle. Owners with third-party cover only had no claim to make on their own vehicles. An organization that does not know which of its vans and pool cars fall into that group will find out at the worst moment.

The second is the "natural disaster" exclusion. The comprehensive form excludes losses from a natural disaster, which the policy defines as a widespread natural event the competent authority has declared as such. No authority declared the April 2024 rain a natural disaster, so the exclusion did not apply and comprehensive policies responded. The regulator said so in April 2024; the national insurance federation repeated it in December 2025, noting that the same test applied to the 2025 rains.

When a car is a write-off, the unified policy's rule is that repair costing more than half the vehicle's pre-accident value makes it a total loss, settled at the agreed insured value less twenty per cent depreciation and pro-rated for the policy period. For a fleet, that haircut is the number to put in the plan.

One more thing changed afterwards. Motor rates rose by twenty to fifty per cent within weeks, by one aggregator's account, electric vehicles by close to double, and at least one comprehensive renewal was reported to exclude floods entirely. By April 2026, guidance for motorists said flood cover was often available only as a natural-perils add-on. Check which you have.

## Why claims were declined

Claims were declined for what people did, not for the weather. From late April 2024 insurers sorted claims by circumstance and refused some under the standard negligence exclusion where the vehicle had been driven into standing water or through a flooded underpass. Guidance republished after the March 2026 rains listed the same tests: a car parked properly, with reasonable precautions, is covered; a car driven into a flooded underpass, left in a spot known to flood, or restarted while wet may not be; a police report and an inspection at an approved workshop are required. In December 2025 the industry federation added that a car taken deliberately into a wadi or flood channel is not covered as a natural hazard, comprehensive policy or not.

No count of declined claims was ever published, and the regulator's only public position was that disputes go to Sanadak. For an organization, the practical point is that the moment a driver decides to move a vehicle in a storm is an insurance decision as much as a safety one. The instruction that protects both is short: do not drive through water, do not restart a wet vehicle, photograph where it was parked.

## What was covered: property, contents, stock and tenants

Property was the larger loss and the least insured. By August 2024 an aggregator reported a sharp rise in general claims since April, mostly flood-related, and about a fifth more enquiries about warehouse cover from shop owners, but few actual purchases. Standard commercial cover was described as a property all-risks policy covering water and rain damage alongside fire and theft, with stock, fixtures and equipment insured only if declared, and business interruption available as an extension. For homes, the regulator's position was that structural damage is covered only under a comprehensive policy, and insurers were reported rejecting home claims that lacked photographic evidence.

If you are a tenant, three documents decide who pays for a flooded basement, and they interact. Press explainers after both the 2024 and 2026 rains said the landlord carries the main structural repair cost unless the lease says otherwise, and that tenants are responsible for their own contents. In Dubai, from early May 2024, the real-estate regulator allowed owners' associations to draw on their reserve funds for urgent common-area repairs, because insurance disbursals had historically taken four to six months, and some associations found their funds already in deficit from unpaid service charges. A tenant organization in a tower should read its lease, ask about the building's policy, and insure its own contents and stock separately, before the season rather than after.

## Business interruption

Business-interruption cover in the UAE was described as an extension to a property all-risks policy that pays lost revenue after insured physical damage to the insured's own premises. That last phrase matters. Losing trade because the road outside was flooded or the mall car park was closed, with your own premises dry, does not fit that description. Ask your broker in writing what your policy says about loss of access, rather than assume it covers it.

The industry expected a large number of business-interruption claims, and a reinsurance broker said in June 2024 that the complex property claims with an interruption element would take time to resolve. Developers offered free repairs in their own communities, and a Sharjah master developer housed up to three thousand displaced residents. No reported UAE business-interruption dispute and no reported claim against a car-park operator after April 2024 was found in the sources we searched; if there is a pattern there, it is not in the public record yet.

## What to do before the season

- Ask your broker, in writing, which vehicles are third-party only, whether flood is covered or sits in an add-on, what the write-off formula will pay, and what the business-interruption trigger is.
- Declare the stock, fixtures and equipment you would claim for.
- Read the lease. Know whether the building has a reserve fund.
- Write the driver instruction and the "first hour" evidence list: photographs before anything is dried, moved or restarted; police report; receipts for towing and emergency works; no non-urgent repair without the insurer's approval.
- Keep the policies and the photographs somewhere dry.
- Then test it. A ninety-minute tabletop exercise that ends with the letter from the insurer will tell you, while it is still dry, which of these your plan actually covers.
